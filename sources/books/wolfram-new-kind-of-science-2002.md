# A New Kind of Science — Wolfram (2002)

> A massive, ambitious work arguing that simple computational rules (cellular automata) generate the complexity found in nature, and that computation rather than mathematics is the fundamental language of science.

## Orientation
Wolfram spent a decade studying cellular automata and concluded that even extremely simple rules can produce behavior of irreducible complexity. The book introduces the "principle of computational equivalence," claiming that most sufficiently complex systems perform computations of equivalent sophistication. It is controversial but influential in complexity and computation circles.

## Key Ideas
- Simple rules (like Rule 30) can produce apparently random, complex output
- Computational irreducibility: some systems can only be understood by running them forward — no shortcut exists
- Principle of computational equivalence: complex systems in nature are all computationally equivalent
- Emergence of complex behavior from minimal rule sets
- Wolfram positions computation as the foundational metaphor for science
