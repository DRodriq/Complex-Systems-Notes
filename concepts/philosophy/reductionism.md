# Reductionism

> The explanatory program of explaining higher-level phenomena by decomposing them into their components and deriving their behavior from lower-level laws — the dominant methodology of 20th-century physics, and the primary target of complexity science's critique.

## Orientation

Reductionism is not a single position but a family: ontological reductionism (everything is ultimately physics), explanatory reductionism (phenomena are best explained at the lowest level), and methodological reductionism (break systems into parts and study each). Complexity science does not typically challenge ontological reductionism but argues against explanatory and methodological reductionism for complex systems.

## Core

### Philip Anderson's Critique

The pivot point for the complexity science relationship with reductionism is Philip Anderson's "More is Different" (1972). Anderson argued that at each level of complexity, genuinely new principles apply that cannot be derived from the level below — not because the lower level is wrong, but because the derivation from lower to higher involves broken symmetries and emergent order that are not visible from the constituents.

Anderson's examples: superconductivity cannot be understood by studying individual electrons; the right-left asymmetry of living things cannot be derived from the symmetric equations of physics (spontaneous symmetry breaking); the structure of solids requires concepts (crystal symmetry groups) that are absent from atomic physics. Each new level of complexity requires its own conceptual vocabulary.

This is a pragmatic rather than ontological claim: the lower level is not wrong, just insufficient for explaining higher-level phenomena efficiently or insightfully.

### Weak and Strong Anti-Reductionism

The weak anti-reductionist position (Anderson's): each level requires its own principles, but the levels are causally connected and ontologically continuous. Physics is not violated; it is just not the most illuminating description.

The strong anti-reductionist position (some readings of Kauffman and emergence): higher-level phenomena have genuine downward causal power that is not reducible to lower-level dynamics. This is philosophically contentious and empirically difficult to demonstrate.

Complexity science generally operates in the weak position: study the appropriate level for the phenomena you care about, use the mathematical tools suited to that level, but don't claim the lower level is wrong or disconnected.

### The Hierarchy of Sciences

The traditional view (Oppenheim-Putnam 1958): sciences form a hierarchy from physics through chemistry, biology, psychology, to social science, with each level reducible in principle to the one below. Complexity science challenges this by showing that higher levels are not just summarizations of lower-level descriptions — they involve qualitative novelty that requires new mathematics and new concepts.

## Connections

**Emergence** (`concepts/philosophy/emergence.md`) — emergence is the positive claim that accompanies anti-reductionism: higher-level properties are real and require independent description.

**Complex adaptive systems** (`concepts/complexity/complex-adaptive-systems.md`) — CAS theory is premised on the irreducibility of the agent-interaction-emergence level to component descriptions.

## Open Questions
