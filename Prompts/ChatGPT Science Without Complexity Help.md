**Response 1**
Great question — you’re digging into the intellectual background that _Hooker_ is situating complexity against. Let me unpack a bit:

---

### The "First Paradigm" of Science

What Hooker is pointing to is essentially **the Newtonian paradigm** (17th–19th century physics) as it became generalized as a _model of science itself_. Its assumptions include:

1. **Reductionism** — Complex wholes can be fully explained by breaking them into parts and analyzing those parts in isolation.
    
2. **Linearity** — Causes and effects scale proportionally; small changes yield small effects.
    
3. **Equilibrium / Stability** — Systems either have fixed equilibria or fluctuate around them in ways stable under perturbation.
    
4. **Invariance as Reality** — What’s “real” is what doesn’t change (symmetry groups in formal theory, invariants in dynamical behavior).
    
5. **Determinism** — With enough information about initial conditions, the system’s future can be exactly predicted.
    

This is not Hooker’s invention; it’s a **common reconstruction of “classical science”** in history and philosophy of science. It was canonized by philosophers like **Pierre Duhem, Ernst Mach, and later logical positivists**, and critiqued heavily in the 20th century by figures like **Norbert Wiener, Ilya Prigogine, Herbert Simon, and Philip Anderson** (“More Is Different,” 1972).

---

### Early Failures / Challenges

Hooker mentions Poincaré and the **three-body problem** (1880s–90s), which showed deterministic chaos long before chaos theory had a name. Other classic cracks in the paradigm include:

- **Thermodynamics & Irreversibility (19th century):**  
    Newtonian mechanics was reversible, but heat flow and entropy (Clausius, Boltzmann) introduced time-asymmetry that linear reductionism struggled to handle.
    
- **Statistical Mechanics:**  
    Boltzmann and Gibbs had to abandon strict reductionism, since macroscopic properties (temperature, pressure) emerged statistically, not linearly from microscopic mechanics.
    
- **Electromagnetism (Maxwell, 1860s):**  
    Introduced field concepts that couldn’t be reduced to particle collisions alone.
    
- **Turbulence in Fluids:**  
    Euler and Navier–Stokes equations were known since the 18th century, but solutions were intractable; turbulence resisted linear, reductionist treatment and remains a canonical case of complexity.
    
- **Biology (Darwin, Mendel, later early genetics):**  
    Evolution and heredity resisted neat mechanistic reduction; organisms exhibited emergent properties across levels (organisms, populations, ecosystems).
    
- **Quantum Mechanics (early 20th century):**  
    Undermined determinism, locality, and classical causal notions altogether. Scientists like Bohr, Heisenberg, and Schrödinger were frustrated by the collapse of the classical paradigm.
    

---

### Scientists Who Expressed Frustration

- **Poincaré** — Three-body problem and sensitivity to initial conditions (precursor of chaos).
    
- **Boltzmann** — Struggled with the statistical nature of entropy; ridiculed for departing from strict determinism.
    
- **Prigogine (later)** — Explicitly framed himself as opposing the “classical paradigm,” emphasizing dissipative structures, irreversibility, and far-from-equilibrium systems.
    
- **Anderson (“More Is Different”)** — Critiqued reductionism in physics, arguing for emergence and hierarchy of sciences.
    
- **Wiener (Cybernetics, 1940s)** — Focused on feedback, control, and communication in ways irreducible to linear mechanics.
    

---

### Is Hooker’s Characterization "Common"?

Yes — though expressed in different terms, it’s widely acknowledged in philosophy of science and complexity studies that:

- Classical physics provided a **reductive-linear-deterministic paradigm** of science.
    
- This paradigm was highly successful but broke down outside its “sweet spot” (simple or near-equilibrium systems).
    
- The “paradigm” became an intellectual straightjacket until new frameworks (nonlinear dynamics, statistical mechanics, systems theory, etc.) were built.
    

Philosophers like **Thomas Kuhn, Nancy Cartwright, and John Dupré** also discuss the limits of reductionism and linear causality, though not always under the banner of “complexity.”
