# Why Complexity

> The case for revisiting complexity science as a unified project — both as an intellectual position and as the most direct way to ground the AI era's "we built it but don't understand it" predicament.

## Personal View

There is a quote (possibly apocryphal, possibly wrongly attributed) by Stanislaw M. Ulam that I think about a lot: using the term *nonlinear science* is like referring to the bulk of zoology as the study of *non-elephant* animals. The intuition behind it is that "traditional" sciences and mathematics aren't just the tip of the iceberg in explaining the richness of the universe — the lens you're forced to look through when studying them actively constrains the world into something it isn't. Our quantitative methods have been confined to the linear, symmetrical, and at-or-near equilibrium. That paradigm is the domain of conventional intelligence, leaning hard on reductionism and logical reasoning.

Year by year we contend more with the limits of that lens. Year by year, those our culture calls geniuses fail to apply conventional reasoning to problems of great complexity — often with absurd results, massive waste, unforeseen consequences, or outright backfiring of the original intention. And now we see an unprecedented investment of capital into technologies we don't understand, that are nonetheless succeeding in areas conventional science couldn't crack. That gap is not merely a curiosity. It is fueling waste, stressing societal and environmental systems at a critical time, and foreclosing insights that could have profound impact across all of human understanding.

**Understanding complexity is the key to the next great paradigm shift in human understanding.**

## Why Complexity in AI

Complexity science as a discrete or unified research area has significantly fragmented. Very little current work flies the flag of "complexity" outright. Part of that is the symptom of success — early avenues turned into fruitful, better-defined topics in math and science. But the most provocative questions raised (emergence, self-organization, adaptation), once tackled openly by serious scientists, have largely retreated back into philosophy. Anyone interested in the banner today — after reading the late-2000s/early-2010s materials and being primed with what looked like a new paradigm capable of grounding mathematics and science for nonlinear, far-from-equilibrium problems — looks around and has to start puzzling out the tributaries the field dispersed into.

With some work, you can trace the academic lineage, the concepts, even much of the language used in fields as varied as network science, computational social science, ecology, AI, systems biology, resilience engineering, healthcare and policy, back to "core" complexity. But complexity itself has fragmented to the point that it's mostly adopted (or invoked) as a *framework* or *lens* to analyze domain-specific systems, not as a science in its own right.

This implicit absorption has only accelerated with the rise of AI. Many of the core concepts that traditional science avoided but complexity targeted directly — emergence, adaptation, high-dimensional interactions — are now embedded in machine learning and large-scale modeling. Deep neural networks function as adaptive systems with dynamics that resist linear-component reduction. Recommender systems and social platforms exhibit network effects and feedback loops straight out of core complexity theory. Even reinforcement learning borrows the language of co-evolution and adaptation. Yet without that framing, we fall back on the default view of deep learning as a black box: architectures trained largely through heuristics, hyperparameter selection an empirical nightmare more like monkeys trying to create Shakespeare than cutting-edge engineering. A framework for self-organization and attractor landscapes could help explain why some architectures stabilize, collapse, or suddenly generalize.

These systems embody complexity without acknowledging it. We rely on emergence without a language to describe it, invoke adaptation without a theory of adaptive dynamics, lean on nonlinearity and high-dimensional interactions without unifying mathematics to make sense of them. The result: one of the most ambitious engineering projects in human history advances largely through brute-force heuristics, empirical tinkering, and post-hoc interpretability. Explainable AI — the retroactive attempt to say why a system produced a given output — is perhaps the clearest symptom of the deficit. Never before have we built machines of such power with so little principled understanding of their internal dynamics.

As a practical aside: we are pouring what will end up being trillions of dollars into data center infrastructure, largely driven by AI demand, and are strikingly unable to ask the most basic engineering questions. *How many resources are actually needed? What are the performance requirements? What should the final product look like?* Standard operating procedure has been: accumulate as much computing power as possible, funnel whatever data can be bought, cheated, or stolen through it, and hope the resulting model "sounds about right."

This is exactly where complexity science could re-enter the conversation. A general framework for emergence and self-organization could move us past ad hoc explanations toward structural understanding of when and why distributed architectures stabilize, collapse, or generalize. Attractor landscapes and stability analysis might predict phase transitions in training dynamics. Network theory could clarify information flow and bottlenecks in deep architectures. Adaptation models drawn from evolutionary dynamics might ground reinforcement learning in a broader science of co-evolving systems. Closer to the core: if we view these new AI models as the complex systems they are, and study them in that light, complexity's insights could be brought more fully into the open. Doing so would not only provide tools to analyze architectures, stability, and emergent behaviors, but also create a foundation for building new frameworks around them.

Complexity once offered to be a unifying science or new paradigm but fragmented before it could consolidate. Its most useful quantitative insights live on in disparate, exciting areas of mathematics and the sciences. The concepts march on in a nearly zombified state as we tackle ever more complex and nonlinear issues, while fields like AI struggle without conceptual anchors. **Revisiting complexity now is not an act of nostalgia, but an opening to complete what was left unfinished and to develop the science our most ambitious technologies already presuppose.**

What is missing is not more heuristics, but a rigorous mathematical foundation for complexity — a framework capable of unifying dynamics, emergence, adaptation, and self-organization across domains. Without it, complexity risks the fate of earlier proto-sciences (cybernetics, general systems theory) whose insights were absorbed but whose central project dissolved, the most provocative questions dissolving along with them. With it, complexity could provide the conceptual grounding that AI — and science more broadly — now lacks.
