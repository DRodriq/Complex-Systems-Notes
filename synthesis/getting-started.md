# Getting Started with Complexity

> A practical entry guide for someone wanting to engage with complexity science — three orthogonal paths into the material, an ordered booklist for the canon, and pointers to external introductory resources.

## What Complexity Is, In Brief

The real challenge with the terms *complexity* and *complex systems* is that they are... complex. Over the past century, "complexity" started by absorbing and recombining threads from the emerging *systems sciences* — cybernetics, general systems theory, information theory — along with idiosyncratic special cases at the fringes of physics, biology, mathematics, and computer science.

By the 1980s, particularly with the founding of the [[institutions/santa-fe-institute|Santa Fe Institute]] in 1984, these disparate strands were gathered under the umbrella of *complexity science*, spearheaded by some of the most accomplished scientists of the time. That institutional effort marked the first real attempt to unify, formalize, and distill insights across these varied domains.

Since then, however, the terminology has dispersed again, and the star-power of researchers talking about "complexity" has almost completely faded. Relatively little current work flies the flag of "complexity science" or "complex systems" outright. Instead, the ideas have been absorbed into neighboring fields — network science, computational social science, ecology, machine learning, systems biology, resilience engineering. See [[why-complexity]] for the case that this dispersion is itself the central problem to address.

## Three Paths In

There are three useful angles for studying complexity. They aren't mutually exclusive — you'll probably want all three eventually — but knowing which one you're taking at a given moment helps.

### 1. Direct study

Engage with the field under the banner of *complexity* or *complex systems*. Read the traditional texts and classic papers that explicitly framed themselves in those terms — the SFI canon and adjacent work that tried to define and unify the field. Just as important: understand the *history* of how complexity took shape. It wasn't invented whole-cloth — it coalesced as researchers from physics, biology, computer science, economics grew frustrated with the methods available for the problems they faced. The stories and observations of these scientists can be traced through the history of science. The turn to complexity was usually a turn born of necessity — an effort to build new analytical tools and a shared language for systems that couldn't be reduced to simple linear models.

### 2. Study as a cluster of topics

Approach complexity as a constellation of interconnected subjects, both modern and historical. This builds intuition for complexity problems, shows how they surface in different contexts, and provides the tools for analyzing them. [[sources/books/sayama-intro-complex-systems-2015|Sayama's *Introduction to the Modeling and Analysis of Complex Systems*]] (2015) is a useful starting point with this orientation: nonlinear dynamics, game theory, collective behavior, networks, systems theory, pattern formation, evolution and adaptation. These are the most fruitful and legitimate offspring of mainline complexity, many bootstrapped wholesale by the "complexity" generation.

### 3. Study where it emerges in other domains

Approach complexity through the fields where it naturally arises. Ecology, economics, neuroscience, sociology, physics — all rich with complexity problems, and many core complexity researchers themselves came out of these disciplines. In this sense, studying complexity is less about a single unified field and more about learning to recognize and work with complex systems wherever they appear.

## Booklist (Best Read in Order)

1. **[[sources/books/waldrop-complexity-1992|Complexity — The Emerging Science at the Edge of Order and Chaos]]** (Waldrop, 1992)
   An accessible narrative history of how complexity science came together in the 1980s, centered on the founding of SFI. Captures the frustrations scientists felt with traditional methods, and the intuition that a new kind of science was needed.

2. **[[sources/books/gleick-chaos-1987|Chaos — Making a New Science]]** (Gleick, 1987)
   Popular account of chaos theory — nonlinear dynamics, sensitive dependence, strange attractors. Core ideas of chaos feed directly into complexity. But where chaos coalesced into a well-defined science with clear mathematical foundations, complexity has remained more diffuse, resisting a single unifying framework.

3. **[[sources/books/holland-complex-adaptive-systems-1992|Complex Adaptive Systems]]** (Holland, 1992)
   One of the foundational technical texts: adaptive agents, emergent behavior, systems that evolve over time. Holland lays the theoretical groundwork for how local rules give rise to global patterns.

4. **[[sources/books/sayama-intro-complex-systems-2015|Introduction to the Modeling and Analysis of Complex Systems]]** (Sayama, 2015)
   Practical, structured approach to complexity as a cluster of topics. Bridges conceptual understanding and hands-on modeling.

5. **[[sources/books/thurner-intro-complex-systems-2018|Introduction to the Theory of Complex Systems]]** (Thurner, 2018)
   Mathematically rigorous treatment. Networks, information theory, stochastic processes, statistical physics approaches to complex systems. Formal tools for moving beyond intuition and simulation.

6. **[[sources/books/hooker-philosophy-complex-systems-2011|Philosophy of Complex Systems]]** (Hooker, 2011)
   For conceptual depth. Emergence, explanation in complex systems, limits of reductionism. A reflective complement to the technical works.
