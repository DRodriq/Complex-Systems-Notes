## Getting Started with Getting Started
Let’s start with the terms _“complexity”_ and _“complex systems.”_ The real challenge with these terms is that they are... complex. Over the past century, “complexity” started by absorbing and recombining threads from the emerging “systems sciences” — cybernetics, general systems theory, information theory — along with idiosyncratic "special cases" at the fringes of physics, biology, mathematics, and computer science (see: [[Science Before Complexity]]. By the 1980s, particularly with the founding of the Santa Fe Institute in 1984, these disparate strands were gathered under the umbrella of _complexity science_, spearheaded by some of the most accomplished scientists of the time. That institutional effort marked the first real attempt to unify, formalize, and distill insights across these varied domains. 

Since then, however, the terminology has again dispersed, and the star-power of researchers talking about "complexity" has almost completely faded. Relatively little current work flies the flag of “complexity science” or “complex systems” outright. Instead, the ideas have been absorbed into neighboring fields or domains — network science, computational social science, ecology, machine learning, systems biology, and resilience engineering, etc. 

This thread is continued in [[Why Complexity]]. For now, it gives us a jumping off point into a few angles for studying complexity

**1. Direct study.**  
This means engaging with the field under the banner of _“complexity”_ or _“complex systems.”_ It involves reading the traditional texts and classic papers that explicitly framed themselves in those terms — work from the Santa Fe Institute and related institutions that attempted to define and unify the field. Just as important is understanding the history of how “complexity” took shape: it was not invented whole-cloth, but coalesced as researchers from physics, biology, computer science, economics, and other disciplines grew frustrated with the lack of methods and concepts available for the problems they were facing. The stories and observations of these scientists is a thread that can be traced back through the history of science (see: [[Science Before Complexity]]). The turn to _complexity_ was often a turn born of necessity — an effort to create new analytical tools and a shared language for systems that could not be reduced to simple linear models.

**2. Study as a cluster of topics.**  
Another path is to approach complexity as a constellation of interconnected subjects, both modern and historical. This builds intuition for complexity problems, shows how they surface in different contexts, and provides the tools for analyzing them. Hiroki Sayama’s _Introduction to the Modeling and Analysis of Complex Systems_ (2015) offers a helpful starting point: nonlinear dynamics, game theory, collective behavior, networks, systems theory, pattern formation, and evolution and adaptation. These are the most fruitful and legitimate offspring from mainline complexity, many being bootstrapped wholesale by "complexity" scientists.

**3. Study where it emerges in other domains.**  
Finally, complexity can be approached through the fields where it naturally arises. Ecology, economics, neuroscience, sociology, and physics are all rich with complexity problems, and many core complexity researchers themselves came out of these disciplines. In this sense, studying complexity is less about a single unified field and more about learning to recognize and work with complex systems wherever they appear.
## Booklist (Best Read in Order)
**[[Complexity - The Emerging Science at the Edge of Order and Chaos (Waldrop) (1992)]]** by [[Mitchell M. Waldrop]]
An accessible narrative history of how _complexity science_ came together in the 1980s, centered on the founding of the Santa Fe Institute. Captures the frustrations scientists felt with traditional methods, and the intuition that a new kind of science was needed for many of the problems being encountered in various fields by the different founders.
**[[Chaos - Making a New Science (Gleick) (1987)]]** by [[James Gleick]]
A popular science account of chaos theory — nonlinear dynamics, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and the strange attractors that fascinated mathematicians and physicists in the 1970s and 80s. Many of the core ideas of chaos theory — nonlinearity, unpredictability, sensitivity — feed directly into the study of complexity. But whereas chaos coalesced into a well-defined science with clear mathematical foundations, complexity has remained more diffuse, resisting a single unifying framework.
**[[Complex Adaptive Systems (Holland) (1992)]]** by [[John Holland]]
One of the foundational technical texts in complexity, introducing the concept of adaptive agents, emergent behavior, and the idea of systems that evolve over time. Holland’s work lays the theoretical groundwork for understanding how local rules give rise to global patterns in complex systems.
**[[Introduction to the Modeling and Analysis of Complex Systems (Sayama) (2015)]]** by [[Hiroki Sayama]]
A practical and structured approach to complexity as a cluster of topics. Sayama organizes the field into nonlinear dynamics, game theory, collective behavior, networks, systems theory, pattern formation, and evolution and adaptation. It is an excellent bridge between conceptual understanding and hands-on modeling.
**[[Introduction to the Theory of Complex Systems (Thurner) (2018)]]** by [[Stefan Thurner]]
A more recent and mathematically rigorous treatment of complexity theory. Thurner’s book covers networks, information theory, stochastic processes, and statistical physics approaches to complex systems, providing formal tools for readers who want to move beyond intuition and simulation into quantitative analysis.
**[[Philosophy of Complex Systems (Hooker) (2011)]]** by [[Cliff A. Hooker]]
For readers interested in conceptual depth, this collection explores philosophical questions surrounding complex systems. Topics include the nature of emergence, explanation in complex systems, and the limits of reductionist reasoning, providing a reflective complement to more technical studies.
