## Why Complexity

There is a quote (possible apocryphal, possibly wrongly attributed) that I think a lot about, by Stanislaw M. Ulam that using a term like nonlinear science is like referring to the bulk of zoology as the study of non-elephant animals. What this means to me, going back to a long held intuition I've had, is that "traditional" sciences and mathematics are not only the tip of the iceberg in explaining the richness and, well, complexity, of the universe, but the lens you're forced to look through when studying them restricts and constrains the world into something it isn't. Our quantitative studies have been limited to the linear, symmetrical, and at or near equilibrium. This paradigm of our sciences and engineering is the domain of conventional intelligence that greatly utilizes reductionism and logical reasoning.

Year by year we contend more with the limits of thinking in these clean linearities and symmetries. Year by year we see those our culture declares as geniuses fail to apply conventional reasoning and traditional methods at problems of great complexity, often with absurd results and massive amounts of waste, unforeseen consequences, or outright backfiring of the original intention. And now, we see an unprecedented investment of capital into creating technologies we don't understand that are having some success in areas that couldn't be so easily solved. The lack of understanding not only ignores some potentially incredible insights that could be made in extremely interesting domains - generalizations that would have profound impacts across all of human understanding - it is fueling massive amounts of waste and irresponsibly stressing various societal and environmental systems at a critical time.

I think understanding complexity is the key to the next great paradigm shift in human understanding.
### Why Complexity in AI

“Complexity” and “complex systems” as a discrete or unified research area has significantly fragmented. Very little current work flies the flag of complexity science outright. While part of this is perhaps a symptom of success, with early avenues of exploration turning into fruitful and better defined topics in math and science, the most provacative questions raised (emergence, self-organization, and adaptation, to name a few), once tackled openly by serious scientists, have retreated again into philosophy. Someone interested in the banner of complexity today, after treating the various materials of the late 2000s and early 2010s and being primed with what offers to be a new paradigm that could build a foundation for math and sciences to examine nonlinear, far from equilibrium, and thus incredibly interesting problems, looks around and has to begin puzzling out the various tributaries it turned into. With some work, one can trace the academic lineage, the concepts, even much of the language often used in fields as varied as network science, computational social science, ecology, artificial intelligence, systems biology, resilience engineering, healthcare and policy, to "core" complexity, yet complexity itself has fragmented to the point its mostly adopted (or invoked) as a framework or lens to analyze domain-specific systems.

This implicit absorption has only accelerated with the rise of AI. Many of the core concepts avoided by traditional science but targeted directly by complexity — emergence, adaptation, high-dimensional interactions — are now embedded in machine learning and large-scale modeling. Deep neural networks function as adaptive systems with dynamics difficult to reduce to linear components. Recommender systems and social media platforms exhibit network effects and feedback loops straight out of (core) complexity theory. Even reinforcement learning borrows the language of co-evolution and adaptation. Yet without this framing, we fall back on the default view of deep learning as a black box: architectures trained largely through heuristics, with hyperparameter selection an empirical nightmare that looks more like monkeys trying to create Shakespeare than cutting-edge engineering. A framework for self-organization and attractor landscapes could help explain why some architectures stabilize, collapse, or suddenly generalize.

These systems now embody complexity without acknowledging it. We rely on emergence without a language to describe it, invoke adaptation without a theory of adaptive dynamics, and lean on nonlinearity and high-dimensional interactions without a unifying mathematics to make sense of them. The result is a strange situation: one of the most ambitious engineering projects in human history advances largely through brute-force heuristics, empirical tinkering, and post-hoc interpretability. Explainable AI, the retroactive attempt to say why a system produced a given output, is perhaps the clearest symptom of this deficit. Never before have we built machines of such power with so little principled understanding of their internal dynamics. 

For a practical aside, lets examine the reality of what is an unprecendented investment of capital and realignment of society going on right in front of our eyes. We are pouring what will end up being trillions of dollars into data center infrastructure, largely driven by the demands of AI, and are strikingly unable to ask the most basic engineering questions: How many resources are actually needed? What are the performance requirements? What should the final product look like? Instead, the standard operating procedure has been to accumulate as much computing power as possible, funnel whatever data can be bought, cheated, or stolen through it, and hope that the resulting model “sounds about right.” 

This is precisely where complexity science could re-enter the conversation. A general framework for emergence and self-organization could move us beyond ad hoc explanations toward structural understanding of when and why distributed architectures stabilize, collapse, or generalize. Attractor landscapes and stability analysis might provide tools for predicting phase transitions in training dynamics. Network theory could clarify information flow and bottlenecks in deep architectures. Adaptation models drawn from evolutionary dynamics might ground reinforcement learning in a broader science of co-evolving systems. Closer to the core of the issue: if we view these new AI models as the complex systems they are, and study them in that light, the insights of complexity could be brought more fully into the open. Doing so would not only provide tools to analyze architectures, stability, and emergent behaviors, but also create a foundation for building new frameworks around them.

Complexity once offered to be a unifying science or a new paradigm but fragmented before it could consolidate. Today, its most useful quantitative insights live on in exciting, but disparate, areas of mathematics and scientific domains. The concepts and frameworks march on in a nearly zombified state as we tackle more complex and nonlinear issues across scientific domains, while fields like AI struggle without conceptual anchors. Revisiting complexity now is not an act of nostalgia, but an opening to complete what was left unfinished and to develop the science that our most ambitious technologies already presuppose.

There remains a sense that a genuine “core science” of complex systems is unfinished and urgently needed. What is missing is not more heuristics, but a rigorous mathematical foundation for complexity: a framework capable of unifying dynamics, emergence, adaptation, and self-organization across domains. Without it, complexity risks the fate of earlier proto-sciences (cybernetics, general systems theory) whose insights were absorbed, but whose central project dissolved, the most interesting and provacative questions along with them. With it, complexity could provide the conceptual grounding that AI, and science more broadly, now lacks.
